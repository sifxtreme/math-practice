🚌 What Do We Do With What's Left Over?

New skill — dividing when it doesn't come out even
3rd Grade
Name:  
Date:
HOW IT WORKS
A class of 26 students is going on a trip. Each van holds 6 students. How many vans do they need?
Step 1 — Divide. 26 ÷ 6 = 4 remainder 2.
Step 2 — Say what the numbers mean. 4 vans get completely full. 2 students are still standing there.
Step 3 — Ask: can I leave the leftover behind? No. Those 2 students still have to get on a van.
Step 4 — Answer the question that was asked. They need 5 vans — 4 full ones and 1 with only 2 students in it.
The trap: the answer is not "4 remainder 2." Always go back and re-read what the question asked for. Sometimes you round up (vans, boxes, tables), sometimes you throw the leftover away (how many full boxes did we fill?).

🍕 Adding Fractions That Don't Match

New skill — unlike denominators
4th Grade
Name:  
Date:
HOW IT WORKS
kid2 eats 1/2 of a pizza. Zaid eats 1/3 of the same pizza. How much did they eat together?
Step 1 — Notice the problem. Halves and thirds are different-sized pieces. You cannot add 1 + 1 and get an answer, because the pieces aren't the same size.
Step 2 — Find a size they can both become. Cut everything into sixths. Both 2 and 3 divide into 6.
Step 3 — Rewrite each fraction. 1/2 = 3/6 (multiply top and bottom by 3). 1/3 = 2/6 (multiply top and bottom by 2).
Step 4 — Now add. 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6. The bottom number does not change — you're just counting sixths.
The trap: 1/2 + 1/3 is not 2/5. Never add the bottoms. To find the common bottom, the fastest way is to multiply the two denominators together (2 × 3 = 6) — it always works, even if it isn't always the smallest.

🔑 Answer Key

For the grown-ups — worked steps + what to watch for
Coach
3rd Grade — kid1 (remainders)
Watch for: answering "4 remainder 2" instead of answering the actual question. If he writes the remainder as the answer, don't correct the arithmetic — ask him to re-read the last sentence of the problem out loud. That's the skill.
1. 5 boxes.
34 ÷ 8 = 4 r 2. Four full boxes, 2 books left. Books can't be left behind → 5 boxes (last one has 2).
2. 7 full boxes, 1 cupcake left over.
50 ÷ 7 = 7 r 1. This one asks for completely full boxes, so we do NOT round up: 7 boxes, 1 cupcake left.
3. 5 buses.
45 ÷ 9 = 5 exactly, no remainder. The "read twice" warning is a fake-out — sometimes it just comes out even. 5 buses.
4. 8 tents; 3 kids in the last one.
38 ÷ 5 = 7 r 3. Every kid needs a bed → round up to 8 tents; the 8th holds the leftover 3.
4th Grade — kid2 (unlike denominators)
Watch for: adding the bottom numbers (1/4 + 1/3 = 2/7). If that happens, go back to the pizza picture — you can't count pieces of different sizes. Also watch for him converting one fraction and forgetting the other.
1. 7/12 of the book.
Common bottom 4 × 3 = 12. 1/4 = 3/12, 1/3 = 4/12. 3/12 + 4/12 = 7/12.
2. 5/6 cup.
6 already works (3 divides into 6). 2/3 = 4/6. 4/6 + 1/6 = 5/6. Good one to point out that you don't always need to multiply the bottoms.
3. 23/20 miles = 1 3/20 miles — more than 1 mile.
Common bottom 4 × 5 = 20. 3/4 = 15/20, 2/5 = 8/20. 15/20 + 8/20 = 23/20, which is 1 whole and 3/20. Since 23 > 20, it's more than a mile.
4. 13/24 of the jug.
Common bottom 8 × 3 = 24. 7/8 = 21/24, 1/3 = 8/24. 21/24 − 8/24 = 13/24. Same procedure as adding — only the last step changes.
