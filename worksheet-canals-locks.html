SHOW YOUR THINKING
Answer:
Did you know? One canal barge could carry as much grain as dozens of horse-drawn wagons. That is why old canals were called the highways of their day.
2.
A lock chamber holds 9 boats at a time. 150 boats are waiting to pass through. How many times must the lock fill so that every boat gets through?
SHOW YOUR THINKING
Answer:
Did you know? A lock is a water elevator. It fills or empties to lift a boat between two water levels, using only gravity — no engine.
3.
A keeper counts 172 boats through the north lock and 236 boats through the south lock. She writes them all evenly onto 6 log pages, the same number on each. How many boats go on each page?
SHOW YOUR THINKING
Answer:
Did you know? Some canals cross straight over a river on a bridge made to carry water, called an aqueduct — a boat sails above a boat.
4.
One lock's water tank is 3/4 full. Another lock's tank is 5/7 full. Which tank holds more water?
SHOW YOUR THINKING
Answer:
Did you know? A single big lock can hold millions of litres of water, yet it can fill in under ten minutes.
5.
A rectangular lock chamber has an area of 84 square metres. It is 6 m wide. How long is it, and what is the perimeter of the chamber?
SHOW YOUR THINKING
Answer:
Did you know? The locks on the Panama Canal are long enough to hold a ship as long as three football fields end to end.

A reservoir behind a dam starts 7/8 full. Opening the lock gates drains 1/3 of a full reservoir. What fraction is left?
SHOW YOUR THINKING
Answer:
Did you know? The water a dam holds back can be let out to spin turbines and make electricity for a whole city.

A gauge reads the upstream water level at 4.7 m and the downstream level at 2.85 m. What is the total of the two readings, and how much higher is the upstream level than the downstream one?
SHOW YOUR THINKING
Answer:
Did you know? A keeper watches the upstream and downstream gauges closely. Open the gates with too big a gap and the rush of water can throw the boats around.

SHOW YOUR THINKING
Answer:
Did you know? The world's busiest lock passes tens of thousands of boats a year, running day and night without stopping.

🔑 Answer Key

For the grown-ups — worked steps included
Coach
3rd Grade — Musa
1. 714 sacks still on the water.
116 × 8 = 928, then 928 − 214 = 714. Multiply first, subtract second — 116 − 214 going negative is the give-away that the order slipped.
2. 17 fills.
150 ÷ 9 = 16 remainder 6. Those 6 leftover boats still need a fill, so round up to 17. "16" is the answer that ignores the question.
3. 68 boats per page.
172 + 236 = 408, then 408 ÷ 6 = 68. Add both locks before splitting. Dividing 172 by 6 alone forgets the south lock.
4. The 3/4 tank.
Twenty-eighths: 3/4 = 21/28 and 5/7 = 20/28, so 3/4 is more. They are close on purpose — this one cannot be eyeballed.
5. Length 14 m; perimeter 40 m.
84 ÷ 6 = 14 m (the missing length). Perimeter = 2 × (6 + 14) = 40 m. Given area, asked perimeter: divide for the side, don't halve.
Watch for: Cumulative — five different skills on one sheet, no scaffolds. #2 is the remainder-interpretation one: expect "16" and ask what happens to the last 6 boats. #4 is deliberately close (21/28 vs 20/28) so the bigger bottom number can't be read as "more". #5: halving 84 to get 42 is the classic slip — it must be a divide by the known side.
4th Grade — Yusuf
1. 1,728 crates.
64 × 27 = (64 × 20) + (64 × 7) = 1,280 + 448 = 1,728. Two-digit by two-digit: the second partial row shifts one column left.
2. 326 m per shift, 7 m left over.
2,615 ÷ 8 = 326 remainder 7. Check: 326 × 8 + 7 = 2,615. ✓ The question asks for both numbers.
3. 13/24 of the reservoir left.
Twenty-fourths: 7/8 = 21/24 and 1/3 = 8/24. 21/24 − 8/24 = 13/24. Unlike denominators — a common bottom number first, then subtract the tops.
4. Total 7.55 m; upstream is higher by 1.85 m.
Write 4.7 as 4.70 and line the points up. 4.70 + 2.85 = 7.55; 4.70 − 2.85 = 1.85. The padding has to be written, not just thought.
5. 234 boats — 33 full groups, 3 left over.
Rate first: 156 ÷ 6 = 26 per hour. Then 26 × 9 = 234. Then 234 ÷ 7 = 33 r 3. Check: 33 × 7 + 3 = 234. ✓
Watch for: Cumulative, no scaffolds anywhere. #4 is the decimal-padding check — an answer of 7.55 with no 4.70 written down is not a pass on these sheets. #5: jumping straight to 156 × 9 skips the per-hour rate and is the most likely wrong turn. #2 and #5 both want the remainder reported, not dropped.
